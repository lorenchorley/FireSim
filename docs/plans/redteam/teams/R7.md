# R7 (blind reviewer): should FireSim's solver be ported to C#? Prototype plan, failure modes, first check

Isolation statement: I did not open `docs/plans/dotnet-option-b-prototype.md`, `docs/plans/dotnet-option-b-redteam.md`, anything under `prototypes/dotnet-b/`, or `firesim-dotnet.html`. Their names showed up in two directory listings (`ls docs/plans`, `ls` of the repository root), and I did not read any of them. I did not open any other team's file under `redteam/`.

## Answer in brief
The repository says the speed a C# solver would buy is not needed today. One phone ran the 3-D step in 41 ms, and the limit is 198.4 ms. One desktop thread runs 10 m cells at 77 times real time. A port also brings three costs:
- **Matching results:** a C# solver cannot give the same bytes as the TypeScript one unless its maths library and the way it rounds numbers are ported too.
- **Snapshot transfer:** each fire picture (a snapshot of 2.3 MB at 30 m) has to cross a WebView bridge. The bridges that MAUI and Blazor provide carry strings and JSON.
- **A second solver:** the browser, the PWA and *Continue on this device* still need a solver that runs as JavaScript.

The cheapest way to decide starts with a step that needs no engineering: the owner runs the speed test already in the app on their phone. Next comes about one day of porting a single kernel on Linux. The full port should be attempted only after the phone shows a speed gain of at least 1.5 times and a working binary bridge.

## Findings

F1. The premise is unproven: no measured shortfall exists that a faster solver would fix.
Severity: BLOCKER   Confidence: HIGH   Lens: R7
Claim: Every measurement in the repository has the TypeScript solver inside its budget on both phone and desktop. A C# port that only adds speed would not change anything the user sees, unless the owner's own phone falls back to the fast tier.
Evidence: `README.md:411-413`: "Only one phone so far: on it the 3-D step took 41 ms ... the limit of about 198 ms is 4.8 times that." `README.md:400-406`: Auto keeps the 3-D air unless the median step is over 198.4 ms. `docs/plans/air-slice-wind-detail-honest-speed.md:1012`: "One thread already does 10 m ... 10 m 47.0 s (77x)". Line 1018: "the speed the desktop already has is more than a 10 m run needs ... Threads, WebAssembly and WebGPU are not worth starting for speed on this evidence".
Why it matters: weeks would go into a port whose benefit is zero at the current settings. The real question is then about developer preference, which a benchmark cannot answer.
Plan change: add Stage 0 (below). The owner runs *Run the speed test* and copies the debug statistics from the existing APK. The owner also names any reason other than speed. Stop the solver port if the Standard tier's steady median step is at most 66 ms (three times under the limit) and no other reason is given.
Already covered?: no (plan not seen; D34 already rejected the idea for maintenance reasons).

F2. An equivalence check of "byte-identical to TypeScript" fails on day one if the port calls System.Math. A tolerance check is meaningless because the model is chaotic.
Severity: BLOCKER   Confidence: HIGH   Lens: R7
Claim: Two separate problems:
- .NET's transcendental functions call the platform's C runtime (glibc on the Linux desktop, bionic on Android). V8 uses its own port of fdlibm. So C# results differ from TypeScript in the last bits, and the C# phone build differs from the C# desktop build.
- The ember random stream goes through `Math.log`, `Math.cos` and `Math.exp`, and small differences grow into differences of 15 to 150 % in burnt area. A check on final outputs within some tolerance therefore cannot tell a porting bug from noise.
Evidence:
- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.math.exp (updated 2026-10-16, net-11.0): "This method calls into the underlying C runtime, and the exact result or valid input range may differ between different operating systems or architectures."
- Chromium `third_party/fdlibm/README.chromium` (search summary, secondary): V8's math is a modified NetBSD fdlibm, "useful when mathematical consistency across OS platforms is desired".
- `src/core/rng.ts`: `return Math.sqrt(-2 * Math.log(u)) * Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * v);` and `median * Math.exp(sigmaLn * this.normal())`.
- `studies/desktop-simulation/README.md` determinism table: changing only the seed moves burnt area from 2482.83 to 2882.34 ha (16 %).
- Plan line 1016: embers off against embers on gives 1,002 against 2,483 ha. The ember cap alone moves area "by tens of percent".
Why it matters: without bit-identity, every porting bug hides inside seed noise. The port's correctness then needs runs over many seeds for every scenario, and every future change to the solver needs a statistical review.
Plan change: make bit-identity the correctness gate. Port V8's `ieee754` functions (exp, log, pow, sin, cos, atan2, tanh and the others in use) into C#, and use them in place of System.Math. Gate in two places: Node against .NET on Linux x64, and the WebView against .NET on the phone (arm64). Only if this fails, fall back to a statistical check: at least 20 seeds per engine, burnt-area distributions compared. Report that the fallback is the price of the port.
Already covered?: no.

F3. A direct translation to C# silently changes results through float32 arithmetic, rounding, sorting and iteration order. I reproduced the first.
Severity: MAJOR   Confidence: HIGH   Lens: R7
Claim: Four differences between JavaScript and C#:
- **float32 arithmetic:** JavaScript computes in double and rounds only when it stores into a `Float32Array`. C# `float` operations round after every step.
- **Rounding:** JavaScript `Math.round` rounds halves up. C# `Math.Round` rounds halves to even by default.
- **Sorting:** C# `Array.Sort` is unstable.
- **Iteration order:** JavaScript `Map` and `Set` iterate in insertion order. C# `Dictionary` and `HashSet` do not promise any order.

The solver uses all four.
Evidence:
- I reproduced the app's frozen kernel K1 (`src/ui/speedTestKernels.ts`). With double maths and float32 stores it gives checksum 2099675145 = 0x7d268009, which equals `FROZEN.stencilAfter3` in `src/ui/speedTest.test.ts:26`. With float operations, as a naive C# port would do them, it gives 441721657, and 26,874 of 40,500 cells differ after 3 sweeps. Script: `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-FireSim/3863bd8b-64fd-5360-ae9d-8f33436d6372/scratchpad/r7/k1.mjs`.
- Counts in the solver folders, excluding tests: 868 `Float32Array`/`Float64Array` references, 141 `Math.round(`, 43 `.sort(`, 11 `new Map`/`new Set` in fire, embers, atmosphere and simulation. Example: `src/embers/EmberModel.ts:1208` `pri.sort((a, b) => this.age[a]! - this.age[b]! || b - a)`.
- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.array.sort: "This implementation performs an unstable sort".
- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.math.round (updated 2026-10-16): the default is "Rounding to nearest even".
Why it matters: these four traps produce differences that look like the noise in F2. They would be found late and expensively.
Plan change: write porting rules (load float as double, store as float; a `JsRound` helper; stable sorts; maps that keep insertion order). Make the K1 checksum 0x7d268009 the first gate the C# code must pass.
Already covered?: no.

F4. The stock MAUI and Blazor Hybrid bridges carry strings or JSON. A snapshot is 2.3 MB at 30 m and 16 MB at 10 m, so the bridge, not the solver, may set the speed.
Severity: MAJOR   Confidence: MEDIUM   Lens: R7
Claim: MAUI HybridWebView's paths from .NET to JavaScript are JSON strings, run as script. Blazor Hybrid's IPC serialises each message as a JSON string, and System.Text.Json writes `byte[]` as base64 (that last point is my inference). Android does have a binary ArrayBuffer path through androidx.webkit, but it comes with a warning about running out of memory, and neither framework uses it.
Evidence:
- https://source.dot.net/Microsoft.Maui/Handlers/HybridWebView/HybridWebViewHandler.cs.html (current main): parameters go through `JsonSerializer.Serialize(...)` and are sent "through the `EvaluateJavaScriptAsync` command". Raw messages are pipe-delimited strings.
- https://github.com/dotnet/aspnetcore/blob/main/src/Components/WebView/WebView/src/IpcCommon.cs: `return $"{_ipcMessagePrefix}{JsonSerializer.Serialize(messageTypeAndArgs, ...)}"`.
- https://developer.android.com/reference/androidx/webkit/JavaScriptReplyProxy: `postMessage(byte[])`, added in 1.8.0 and gated on `WEB_MESSAGE_ARRAY_BUFFER`: "Be aware that large byte buffers can lead to out-of-memory crashes on low-end devices."
- Sizes: plan line 1012 ("one snapshot 2.3 MB at 30 m, 16 MB at 10 m"). One snapshot is sent every 60 simulated seconds (`src/core/types.ts:683`).
- Today the Web Worker hands snapshot buffers over without copying: `src/sim/host.ts:194` `snapshotTransferables(s)`.
Why it matters: the WebView today moves snapshots for nothing. A C# solver turns every snapshot into one or more copies, and possibly into text. If the bridge costs more than the solver saves, the port loses on the phone.
Plan change: measure the bridge on the phone in Stage 2, before any port. Compare three paths: (a) HybridWebView JSON, (b) `addWebMessageListener` with an ArrayBuffer, (c) an intercepted request that returns the frame bytes. Send the existing codec frame (`src/sim/remote/codec.ts`). Pass: p95 at most 25 ms per 2.3 MB frame from .NET to a `Float32Array` in the page, with no main-thread task over 50 ms at 5 frames a second.
Already covered?: no.

F5. Porting "the solver" means porting about 40,000 lines plus about 21,000 lines of tests, including the explain engine. The code is changing quickly.
Severity: MAJOR   Confidence: HIGH   Lens: R7
Claim: The worker owns the simulation, the explain engine (the insight cards and *Why here?*), the forecast insights and the fuel model. All of them would have to move to C#, because they read solver state that would no longer exist in JavaScript.
Evidence:
- `src/sim/simulation.ts:95-114` imports `../fuel`, `../explain`, `../scenario/weather`, `../scenario/windDirection`, `../terrain` and others.
- `src/sim/protocol.ts`: `explain` and `ready: forecastInsights` are worker messages.
- Line counts without tests: fire 6,729; embers 4,028; atmosphere 6,260; sim 5,251; terrain 1,823; core 3,737; fuel 5,009; explain 7,075. Tests: about 21,200 lines.
- Explain is 5.0 % of fast-tier time (studies table "explain: the insight cards").
- `git log --since=2026-10-01 -- src/fire src/atmosphere src/embers src/sim`: 31 commits.
Why it matters: a slice that ports only the hot kernels says nothing about port cost. During a long port, the TypeScript solver keeps changing, and the two drift apart before the C# one is finished.
Plan change: Stage 3 measures how fast the port goes (lines ported and tested per engineer-day, defects found by the bit-identity gate), on a vertical slice that includes the explain engine stubbed behind an interface. Extrapolate the total cost, and require the owner to freeze solver changes or accept two solvers for the duration.
Already covered?: no.

F6. A C# solver in the MAUI app does not make one solver. The web, PWA and desktop-served pages still need a JavaScript solver, so the result is two solvers (D34) or Blazor WebAssembly on Mono.
Severity: BLOCKER   Confidence: HIGH (repository) / MEDIUM (WebAssembly speed)   Lens: R7
Claim: Three paths run the solver in a browser:
- the app in a desktop browser or PWA;
- the page a phone's browser opens from the desktop host;
- *Continue on this device*, which preloads a solver worker script to run offline.

Either all three keep the TypeScript solver, which means two solvers and goes against D34, or they run C# compiled to WebAssembly. Blazor WebAssembly still runs on Mono (an IL interpreter, or AOT), and its speed against V8 is not measured anywhere.
Evidence:
- `desktop/README.md:396-398`: the page "makes this device's own engine, idle, and keeps it for the run (one idle worker, whose script, 484,365 bytes ... is fetched from the host)".
- D34 (plan line 121): "no second solver (no C# or .NET port ...) ... set aside because it means maintaining two solvers".
- https://devblogs.microsoft.com/dotnet/coreclr-progress-and-mono-timeline-dotnet-maui/ (2026-07-14): "Blazor WebAssembly still uses Mono."
- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/aspnet/core/blazor/webassembly-build-tools-and-aot (search summary): without AOT "apps run ... using a .NET IL interpreter".
Why it matters: the owner's real choice is between two solvers and a WebAssembly solver of unknown speed. A prototype that measures only MAUI on a phone hides that choice.
Plan change: Stage 1 also runs the C# kernels as `browser-wasm` with AOT in headless Chromium (Playwright is already in the repository). Pass: at least 0.8 times V8's speed. Otherwise the decision memo must say "two solvers" explicitly.
Already covered?: no.

F7. The phone has one 41 ms reading taken during warm-up. .NET 11 on Android is CoreCLR with JIT and partial ReadyToRun, and phones throttle when hot. A comparison made the wrong way will be wrong.
Severity: MAJOR   Confidence: HIGH   Lens: R7
Claim: Comparing a C# number against the 41 ms reading, or C# on its first runs against V8 after warm-up, or runs taken at different temperatures, will give the wrong ratio.
Evidence:
- `README.md:411`: "That was the mean of the first 20 steps, warm-up included".
- https://learn.microsoft.com/dotnet/maui/deployment/runtimes-compilation (ms.date 2026-10-08), .NET 11 Android: "CoreCLR + composite partial ReadyToRun + JIT" in Release; Mono "isn't supported for .NET MAUI apps that target .NET 11+"; NativeAOT "experimental on Android".
- https://developer.android.com/games/optimize/adpf/thermal: "Devices can only maintain a high level of performance for a limited amount of time before being thermally throttled". Do not call `getThermalHeadroom` more than once every 10 s.
Why it matters: the owner can only sideload and report numbers. Each repeat round with the phone costs days.
Plan change: Stage 2 builds one APK that runs both engines (TypeScript in a WebView Worker, C# in-process) in the order A B B A, three times. Each run is 30 s of warm-up and 60 s timed, plus one sustained 10-minute run per engine. Log the thermal headroom every 10 s. A Copy button produces one text block. Pass on the ratio of steady-state medians, and report the ratio while throttled separately.
Already covered?: no.

F8. Faster kernels are diluted by the other phases, and scalar C# is not reliably faster than V8. A gate based only on kernels would overstate the gain.
Severity: MAJOR   Confidence: MEDIUM   Lens: R7
Claim: In the fast tier no single phase dominates: fire 58 %, embers 13 %, coupling 11 %, snapshot 4.5 %, explain 5 %. Public benchmarks show scalar C# close to Node, with large wins only from intrinsics or unsafe code, so the whole-simulation ratio will be smaller than the best kernel's.
Evidence:
- `studies/desktop-simulation/README.md` phase table (fast 30 m: fireStep 37.7 %, firePrepare 15.5 %, embers 12.8 %, fireHeat 8.4 %; Standard: atmosphere 61.5 %).
- Benchmarks Game 25.03 (.NET 9, before .NET 11; it mixes threaded programs, so this is indicative only): spectral-norm, best unflagged C# 1.47 s against Node.js 1.60 s; n-body, C# 3.13 s (flagged for possible hand-written vector instructions) against Node.js 8.55 s. https://benchmarksgame-team.pages.debian.net/benchmarksgame/performance/spectralnorm.html and .../nbody.html.
Why it matters: on its own, a 3 times gain on the elliptic solver gives about 1.7 times for the Standard tier, and much less for the fast tier.
Plan change: gate on a weighted mix that uses the profile's phase shares, and confirm on the Stage 3 whole-simulation slice (at least 1.4 times on Linux).
Already covered?: no.

## The ten most likely ways the plan or the decision fails
1. The port solves a speed problem that does not exist (F1).
2. Its correctness cannot be shown, because the C runtime's maths and the model's chaos rule out comparing outputs (F2).
3. JavaScript and C# differ in float32 arithmetic, rounding, sorting and iteration order (F3).
4. The cost of the bridge cancels the solver's gain on the phone (F4).
5. The port's cost is underestimated, because explain, fuel and forecast are part of "the solver", and the TypeScript solver keeps changing in the meantime (F5).
6. The result is two solvers anyway, or a Blazor WebAssembly solver on Mono of unknown speed, for the web, PWA, desktop-served page and *Continue on this device* (F6).
7. The phone numbers are invalid: one old warm-up reading, JIT tiering and thermal throttling (F7).
8. A gate based on kernels overstates the whole-simulation gain (F8).
9. **The toolchain is new.** .NET 11 was due for release in November 2026 (my search found no release announcement). CoreCLR became the only Android runtime in Preview 6 (blog post, 2026-07-14). NativeAOT on Android is experimental. On Linux only the `maui-android` workload exists, so MAUI on Windows or macOS cannot be built or tested in the container (Microsoft Learn installation page, search summary). This is a reason to keep phone stages small. It is not a finding, because it can only be checked by building something.
10. **The phone loop with the owner wastes rounds.** Without a protocol that reports itself (one APK, one button, one text block to copy), each unclear number costs a sideload cycle. Stage 2 is designed around this.

## What I would check first, and why
1. **Stage 0 (no engineering).** The owner installs the current APK, runs a 6-hour Standard scenario, presses *Run the speed test*, and pastes the debug statistics: the Auto median step, the K1 and K2 rates and the device. This is the only fact that can end the whole question for free. If the phone is at least three times inside the 198.4 ms limit, nothing gets faster in a way the user would notice (F1).
2. **In parallel, one day on Linux.** Port the frozen K1 kernel and `EllipticSolver.relax`, `gradient` and `divergence` (`src/atmosphere/solver.ts`) to C#. Check that the K1 checksum equals 0x7d268009 bit for bit, and measure the speed of the steady state against Node. Two cheap facts come out of it: whether C# can match the TypeScript bytes at all (F2, F3), and whether its speed per thread differs from V8 enough to matter (F8).

## Independent plan

Principle: the cheapest kill first. Each stage has a pass number, a stop number and a reason. No stage needs the one before it to be finished beyond its own gate. Everything runs in the Linux container (4 cores, 16 GB, .NET 11 SDK, `maui-android` workload) except Stages 0 and 2b, which run on the owner's phone.

### Stage 0: does a problem exist? (0.5 engineer-day and 15 minutes of the owner's time; on the phone, with the existing APK)
- **Measure:** on the owner's phone, Katoomba 6 km, Balanced, a 6 h run:
  - the Auto decision and the steady median step (`EngineInfo.autoTune`);
  - the speed test (K1 cell updates a second, K2 GB a second);
  - the simulated seconds per wall second of the fast tier (Saver) and of the Standard tier, from the debug statistics;
  - the thermal state from Android settings, if shown.

  Ask the owner to state any reason other than speed: C# skills, Razor screens, hiring.
- **Pass:** continue to Stage 1 only if (a) the Standard median step is over 66 ms (less than three times headroom under 198.4 ms, so a faster solver keeps the 3-D air on more phones or allows finer settings), or (b) the owner names a speed-bound feature they want on the phone (for example 20 m cells or more embers on the phone).
- **Stop:** the step is at most 66 ms and no speed-bound feature is named. Then the answer is: a C# solver buys nothing measurable. If Razor or MAUI screens are wanted, that is option A (a .NET shell around the TypeScript solver) and needs its own plan.
- **Risks:** a single phone. Mitigation: ask whether a second, older Android phone is available. Without one, label the result as true for one phone only.

### Stage 1: can C# match the TypeScript bytes, and is it faster per thread? (2 to 3 engineer-days, Linux)
- **Kernels, translated directly, with double maths and float32 stores:**
  - K1, the frozen speed-test stencil (`src/ui/speedTestKernels.ts`);
  - `LevelSetCore.stepFixed` and `reinit` (`src/fire/spread/levelSet.ts`), together 26 % of fast-tier CPU at 30 m and over 50 % at 10 m;
  - `PyrogenicPotential.solve` (`src/atmosphere/pyrogenic.ts`, 22 % at 10 m);
  - `EllipticSolver.relax`, `gradient`, `divergence` and `dynamics.advect` on the Standard grid of 45 x 45 x 20 (about 69 % of Standard-tier CPU).

  Inputs come from TypeScript state dumped at t = 1 h of Katoomba 6 km seed 1, at 30 m and 10 m, written as codec frames.
- **Variants:** (a) scalar C#, a direct translation; (b) `Vector128` SIMD where the loop allows it, kept bit-identical to (a) (no reassociated sums); (c) `Parallel.For` over z-slabs or row bands with no parallel reductions, so results stay bit-identical; (d) the scalar code compiled to `browser-wasm` with AOT and run in headless Chromium.
- **Measure:** BenchmarkDotNet steady state against Node 24 (or the repository's Node) with the same inputs, on one pinned core, median of 15. The weighted mix uses the phase shares (fast 30 m, fast 10 m, Standard 30 m).
- **Gates:**
  - **Correctness, zero tolerance:** K1 checksum = 0x7d268009, and every kernel's output identical in bytes to TypeScript. Reason: these kernels use only + - * / and sqrt, which are correctly rounded in both languages. If even they cannot match, the full simulation will not.
  - **Speed:** pass if scalar C# on the weighted mix is at least 1.5 times Node, or SIMD/threads (b, c) is at least 2.5 times while staying bit-identical. Reason: what remains inside D34 (TypeScript worker threads, WebAssembly for one hot loop) reaches about 1.8 to 2.6 times by 15e's Amdahl bounds without a second solver. A port must beat that by more than the noise between phones and runs, which the repository puts at 1 to 3 %, and 20 % under load.
  - **Stop:** scalar under 1.2 times and the best variant under 2 times.
  - **WebAssembly (d):** record the ratio. If it is under 0.8 times V8, the decision memo must say "a C# solver means two solvers" (F6).
- **Risks:** a translation that is faster than the original only because it is optimised differently. Mitigation: variant (a) must be a line-by-line translation, and any optimisation goes in (b) or (c) only. Also, x64 is not arm64, which is why Stage 2 exists.

### Stage 2: the phone, solver speed and bridge cost in one APK (2 to 3 engineer-days, and about 1 hour of the owner's time)
- **2a, build (Linux):** one `net11.0-android` Release APK (CoreCLR, composite partial ReadyToRun, as shipped) holding:
  - the Stage 1 C# kernels;
  - a WebView page that runs the same TypeScript kernels in a Web Worker (the real bundled code);
  - a bridge test harness.

  Use MAUI only if the bridge under test is HybridWebView. Otherwise a plain Android activity removes MAUI as a variable.
- **2b, run (phone):**
  - **Order:** charged above 50 %, airplane mode, 5 minutes of cool-down, then the A B B A sequence three times.
  - **Each run:** 30 s of warm-up and 60 s timed. Then one sustained 10-minute run per engine.
  - **Thermal:** `getThermalHeadroom` every 10 s.
  - **Bridge:**
    - **Frames:** synthetic codec frames of 2.3 MB and 16 MB.
    - **Paths:** (i) HybridWebView `InvokeJavaScriptAsync` (JSON), (ii) `addWebMessageListener` with `JavaScriptReplyProxy.postMessage(byte[])`, (iii) an intercepted request that returns the bytes.
    - **Rates:** 1, 5 and 20 frames a second.
    - **Record:** the time from .NET to a ready `Float32Array` in the page, the page's long tasks (PerformanceObserver), and the dropped frames of a running Three.js scene.
  - **Output:** one Copy button, one text block.
- **Gates:**
  - **Solver:** pass if C# / WebView steady medians on the weighted mix are at least 1.5 times, and at least 1.3 times in the throttled phase. Stop if under 1.2 times.
  - **Bridge:** pass if one path gives p95 at most 25 ms per 2.3 MB frame and no long task over 50 ms at 5 frames a second. 5 frames a second is above what a phone computes at 30 m, roughly 3 snapshots a second at 200 times real time. Stop if only the JSON path works and costs over 100 ms per frame: the bridge then cancels the solver's gain.
- **Risks:** one phone, the owner's procedure, and WebView version differences. The text block includes the WebView version and the device model.

### Stage 3: a vertical slice to price the port and prove the bytes match (at most 2 weeks, Linux, then 1 run on the phone)
- **Scope:**
  - the fast tier with embers on (fire models, level set, the diagnosed wind, pyrogenic coupling, fuel and moisture, ember flight, RNG);
  - the explain engine behind an interface (stubbed);
  - C# ports of V8's `ieee754` functions;
  - porting rules (`JsRound`, stable sorts, maps that keep insertion order, float32 store semantics);
  - snapshots encoded with the existing codec (`src/sim/remote/codec.ts`).
- **Scenario:** Katoomba 6 km, 30 m, seed 1, 2 h, the V20 hot-NW preset.
- **Gates:**
  - all 120 minute-by-minute snapshot hashes identical between Node and .NET on Linux;
  - the last hash identical between the WebView and .NET on the phone;
  - whole-simulation speed at least 1.4 times Node on Linux;
  - port rate measured, and the total extrapolated to about 40,000 source lines and 21,000 test lines.
- **Stop:**
  - bit-identity still not reached 3 days after the slice compiles;
  - or the extrapolated port takes more than the owner's stated budget;
  - or the whole-simulation speed is under 1.2 times.
- **Integration check, cheap:** an ASP.NET Core host speaks wire protocol version 2, so the unchanged page (with `RemoteSimController`) can drive the C# slice on the desktop. This tests the snapshot shape and the UI without MAUI.
- **Risks:** solver changes during the slice. Freeze `src/fire`, `src/embers`, `src/atmosphere` and `src/sim` for its two weeks, or rebase once at the end and count the drift.

### Stage 4: decision memo (1 day)
The memo holds:
- the numbers from Stages 0 to 3;
- the port cost;
- the bridge chosen;
- the WebAssembly ratio, which decides between two solvers and one;
- what happens to the 0.8.0 Node host, the PWA and *Continue on this device*;
- the cost of keeping equivalence afterwards: bit-identity in CI on every solver change, or statistical checks over many seeds.

The owner then decides on D34.

### Order and spend
| Stage | Engineer time | Owner time | Where | Can it end the question? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | half a day | 15 minutes | phone | yes, at the lowest cost |
| 1 | 2 to 3 days | none | Linux | yes, on correctness and speed per thread |

## Checked and found sound
- **A ready binary seam:** the binary wire codec (`src/sim/remote/codec.ts`, a JSON header plus aligned typed-array bodies) and `RemoteSimController` let a C# engine be tested behind the unchanged page with no MAUI work.
- **A frozen, phone-deployed benchmark:** the speed-test kernels are integer-exact, have checksums pinned in a test, and are already in the APK with a Copy button. My JavaScript replica reproduced the pinned K1 checksum 0x7d268009.
- **Determinism of the TypeScript solver on one machine** is documented (`studies/desktop-simulation/README.md`: 240 of 240 snapshots byte-identical). It is a sound reference to port against.
- **Building an APK on Linux** with the `maui-android` workload is supported. MAUI for Windows or macOS is not available on Linux (Microsoft Learn installation page, via search summary).
- **The mobile runtime question is settled:** .NET 11 Android is CoreCLR only (no Mono AOT branch to test) (Microsoft Learn, 2026-10-08; .NET blog, 2026-07-14).

## Could not check
